Control One — The Class: The Particle Count the Room Must Hold

A cleanroom's class is a measured state — a maximum particle concentration the room's air must stay under, at rest and in operation — and the MEP design is what makes that state achievable: air changes, filtration, and airflow pattern are the class, expressed as engineering.

Control Two — The Cascade: Pressure and Airflow, Room to Room

Clean air stays clean by moving in one direction: the pressure cascade keeps each cleaner space positive to the dirtier space beside it, so leakage at every door, seam, and gap flows outward from the product — never inward toward it.

Control Three — The Envelope: The Shell the Systems Must Serve

The cleanroom envelope — walls, ceiling, seals, doors, and every penetration through them — is an MEP component whether the contract calls it one or not: the tightest system design fails through an unsealed conduit, and every crossing is a decision someone must engineer.

Control Four — The Utilities: The Process Behind the Process

Behind every clean process stands a second, invisible plant — purified water, process gases, vacuum, specialty exhaust, clean power — and the MEP scope either engineers that plant to the tool set's real demands or leaves the cleanroom clean, idle, and useless.

Control Five — The Validation: The Proof the Room Keeps Passing

A cleanroom is not finished when it is built; it is finished when it is certified — and it stays finished only under monitoring: particle counts, recovery tests, and pressure records are the room's continuing testimony that the other four controls still hold.
